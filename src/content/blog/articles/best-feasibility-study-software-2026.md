**Direct answer:** There is no single "best" feasibility study software — there are four types: spreadsheet template libraries, generic proforma SaaS, consultant-produced studies, and jurisdiction-native AI engines. The right choice depends on your deal volume, jurisdictional complexity, and whether the output must satisfy a credit committee. Judge any tool against seven questions: auditability, jurisdiction logic, scenario depth, output quality, data privacy, ownership, and pricing at your real volume.

Every vendor's demo looks brilliant. Only three things survive contact with a credit committee: the math, the jurisdiction logic, and the audit trail.

## 1. The four types of tool you'll meet
**Spreadsheet template libraries** sell you a model you run yourself. Full control — but you are the engine: updates, errors and jurisdiction fixes are your problem, and most templates are blind to non-US payment structures. **Generic proforma SaaS** is fast for standard assets but typically US-centric, subscription-locked, and you rent rather than own the model. **Consultants** remain the incumbent alternative: highest credibility, highest cost ($10K+ per study), weeks of waiting, and every revision is another invoice. **Jurisdiction-native AI engines** automate cashflows with local law baked in, add AI market research and one-click scenario shocks, and produce bankable documents in hours — but they vary wildly in auditability, so verify before you trust.

## 2. The seven questions to ask any vendor
1. **Can I see and export every assumption and formula?** If the math is hidden, the output is an opinion, not a model.
2. **Does it model your market's payment structure — native presets where they exist (Dubai Law No. 8, Malaysia HDA, Australia's 10/90) and configurable rules (no escrow, custom splits, staged retentions) everywhere else?** Bolted-on logic fails silently.
3. **How fast can I run a correlated downside case?** Costs +10%, prices −5%, three-month delay — together, not one at a time.
4. **Is the output bankable?** Would a credit committee accept the format, or would your team re-typeset it for a week?
5. **Where does my deal data live, and who can see it?** Confidentiality is fiduciary, not technical.
6. **Do I own the model and the output?** Including white-labelling for advisory work.
7. **What does it cost at my actual volume?** Subscriptions punish quiet quarters; per-report and lifetime models don't.

## 3. Match the tool to your situation
Occasional deals: a good template or a consultant suffices. Steady pipeline across jurisdictions: an engine pays for itself in the first study. US-only standard assets: generic SaaS may be adequate. Advisory mandates under your own brand: you need white-label output and ownership — check before signing anything monthly.

## 4. The pricing math nobody shows you
$500/month is $6,000/year whether you run two studies or twenty. A $10K consultant study buys one answer at one point in time. Per-report and lifetime pricing aligns cost with usage — which matters in a cyclical industry where deal flow comes in waves.

## 5. Red flags that end evaluations
Locked models with no export. "AI" that is a chatbot wrapper over a static spreadsheet. Privacy policies that don't say where data lives. US-only logic sold as global. And free tiers that make exporting your own inputs impossible.

## 6. Run a proper 30-minute trial
Take one real past deal. Input it. Run the correlated downside. Export the full report. Hand it to a colleague and ask: *would this survive committee?* Judge the exported output — never the demo.

## Where most buyers get it wrong
Choosing on demo polish instead of exported documents. Discovering jurisdiction gaps after a deal breaks. Subscribing before knowing their volume. And never asking the data question until a client asks it first.

**Key takeaways:** Four tool types, different jobs · Seven questions before any purchase · Payment-structure logic is the real dividing line · Price against your real volume · Trial the export, not the demo · Committee-ready output is the only proof.

*The best feasibility software isn't the one with the nicest demo. It's the one whose output survives your credit committee.*
